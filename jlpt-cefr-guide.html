JLPT and CEFR: The New Level on Your Score Report, Explained

November 24, 2026

From the December 2025 test, JLPT score reports also show a CEFR level (A1–C2). If you are aiming at work in Japan — including the Specified Skilled Worker route, where B1 enters the picture from April 2027 — this small extra line on your result changes how employers read your Japanese. Here is what it means, based on official announcements.

If you sat the JLPT in December 2025 or later, your score report contains something no previous cohort received: alongside the familiar N5–N1 result, a CEFR level — the A1-to-C2 scale used for European languages — is now shown as a reference. This article explains why the change was made, how to read the two scales together, and why it matters more than it looks if your goal is working in Japan. Everything below is based on official announcements by the JLPT organizers and Japanese government programs; where a detail is a reference correspondence rather than a strict equivalence, it says so. Confirm the latest rules on official pages before making application decisions.

What changed, and when

ItemDetail
What is newA CEFR level (A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2 scale) printed on the JLPT score report as reference information.
From whenTests from December 2025 onward.
What did NOT changeThe JLPT itself: same five levels (N5–N1), same pass/fail, same sections. The CEFR line is additional information, not a new test.
WhyCEFR is the scale employers and governments outside Japan already understand. Showing it makes a JLPT result legible to people who do not know what "N2" means.

How to read JLPT and CEFR together

The JLPT and CEFR measure overlapping but not identical things. The JLPT tests reading and listening (with vocabulary, grammar, and kanji knowledge) — it has no speaking or writing section. CEFR, by contrast, describes overall communicative ability including speaking and writing. That is why the CEFR level on your report is a reference correspondence derived from your JLPT result, not a claim that you were tested on all four skills.

Your goalTraditional JLPT targetHow the CEFR line helps
Everyday travel and basic conversationN5–N4Shows an A1–A2-style foundation to non-Japanese speakers (reference level; see your report for the official correspondence)

Do not convert levels using unofficial charts found on social media. The correspondence that counts is the one printed on your own score report and published by the organizers.

Why B1 matters: Specified Skilled Worker 2

The CEFR line stops being trivia if you are looking at Japan's Specified Skilled Worker (SSW / 特定技能) program. Under the program's announced changes, from April 1, 2027, the Japanese-language requirement connected to the Specified Skilled Worker (ii) — "SSW2" — route references the CEFR B1 level: broadly, the "independent user" threshold, meaning you can handle most everyday and workplace situations in Japanese without support. In practical terms:

  • If SSW2 is your goal, "pass a JLPT level" is no longer the whole target — you should plan your study so that your result corresponds to B1 or above on the score report, per the official requirements in force when you apply.
  • Because the JLPT does not test speaking, candidates who only drill test questions can hit a paper level while still struggling in a workplace conversation. The B1 framing is a reminder that the endpoint is real communication, not just a certificate.
  • Rules for residence statuses are updated by the Immigration Services Agency; treat April 1, 2027 and the B1 reference as the announced framework and verify the exact accepted evidence (which tests and scores qualify) on official pages before you apply.
