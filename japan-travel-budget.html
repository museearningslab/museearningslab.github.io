How Much Does a Trip to Japan Cost? A Realistic Budget Breakdown

November 24, 2026

Written from Japan. Prices in Japan vary a lot by city, season, and travel style, so this guide works with ranges and the thinking behind them — not fake precision.

"How much does a trip to Japan cost?" is the question every visitor asks first, and the honest answer is: it depends mostly on four decisions — where you sleep, how you eat, how far you travel between cities, and how you stay connected. International flights are excluded here; this breakdown covers what you spend on the ground, per person, per day.

All figures below are rough ranges in yen, meant as planning anchors. Tokyo is generally at the expensive end; regional cities are often noticeably cheaper. Peak seasons (cherry blossom, Golden Week, New Year) push accommodation prices up everywhere.

1. Accommodation: the biggest lever

Lodging is usually the largest single cost, and the category with the widest spread:

  • Hostels and guesthouses: dorm beds are often found roughly in the ¥3,000–¥6,000 per night range in big cities, sometimes less in smaller towns. Private rooms cost more but can still undercut hotels.
  • Business hotels: the workhorse of Japan travel. A clean single room with a small desk and bathroom commonly sits somewhere around ¥7,000–¥13,000 per night depending on city and season.
  • Mid-range hotels and ryokan: comfortable hotels rise from there, and a traditional ryokan with dinner and breakfast included is a different price category entirely — treat it as an experience you budget for separately, often one night rather than every night.

Practical note: prices are often quoted per person in ryokan and per room in hotels. Read which one before comparing.

2. Food: cheap is genuinely good in Japan

Japan is unusual in that eating cheaply does not mean eating badly.

  • Breakfast: a convenience-store or bakery breakfast can be a few hundred yen; a café set menu costs more.
  • Lunch: the best value meal of the day. Many restaurants offer lunch sets roughly in the ¥800–¥1,500 range — often the same kitchen that charges much more at dinner.
  • Dinner: a casual dinner might be ¥1,000–¥2,500; a nicer restaurant, sushi, or an izakaya evening with drinks can run ¥3,000–¥6,000 or more per person.

A realistic food budget is roughly ¥3,000–¥5,000 per day if you mix convenience stores, lunch sets, and one proper restaurant meal — and ¥8,000+ per day if food is a main purpose of your trip. Both are legitimate ways to travel; just decide which one you are doing before you arrive.

3. Transport inside Japan: two separate budgets

Within a city, trains and subways are priced per ride, typically a few hundred yen per trip. A rechargeable IC card (or its mobile version) makes this painless. Budget roughly ¥500–¥1,000 per day for city transport unless you are crossing the city repeatedly.

Between cities is where budgets break. A single long-distance Shinkansen ticket can cost more than a night's hotel. Think about:

  • How many long-distance moves your itinerary really needs. One base city with day trips is far cheaper than changing hotels every two days.
  • Whether a rail pass pays off depends entirely on your specific route — compare the pass price against the individual tickets for your actual itinerary rather than assuming.
  • Highway buses are slower but often much cheaper on popular routes, and overnight buses can replace a hotel night.

4. Connectivity: a small cost people forget to plan

You will use data constantly — maps, train routes, translation. Options include a travel eSIM, a physical SIM, pocket Wi-Fi rental, or roaming from your home carrier, and total cost depends on your trip length and data use. It is a small line item compared with hotels, but choose before you go rather than at the airport. We wrote a separate guide on what to check: Best eSIM for Japan Travel: What to Check Before You Buy.

5. Attractions, shopping, and the miscellaneous 10%

Many temples and shrines charge a few hundred yen for entry; major museums and observation decks cost more, and theme parks are a full-day, full-price commitment. Shopping is entirely self-inflicted — decide a souvenir budget in advance if that is a risk for you. As a rule of thumb, adding about 10% on top of your planned total for small unplanned costs (lockers, laundry, a taxi when it rains) keeps the budget honest.

Where travelers actually save money

  • Lunch is the value meal. Eat your main restaurant meal at noon.
  • Fewer bases, more day trips. Every hotel change costs money and half a day.
  • Department store basements and supermarkets in the evening discount prepared food — a genuinely good cheap dinner.
  • Book accommodation early for peak seasons; in Japan, waiting rarely makes lodging cheaper in cherry-blossom season.

Budget for the trip you actually want. Japan rewards both the careful budget traveler and the food-focused spender — the only bad budget is the one you never made.
