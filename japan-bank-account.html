How to Open a Bank Account in Japan as a Foreigner

November 24, 2026

You have landed, registered your address, and now your employer, landlord, or phone carrier wants a Japanese bank account. This is the order that actually works, the documents to bring, and the one rule — the six-month rule — that explains most of the confusing answers you will get at bank counters.

This is general information based on how the system works for most new residents; it is not financial advice. Requirements differ by bank and sometimes by branch, and rules change. Treat the checklist below as preparation, and confirm the final requirements at the counter or on the bank's official site.

Why Japan Post Bank (Yucho) is the realistic first account

For most new arrivals, Japan Post Bank (ゆうちょ銀行, Yucho Ginko) is the practical first account, for three reasons:

  • It will actually open an account for a newcomer. Japan Post Bank opens accounts for people who have just arrived, including the restricted "non-resident" type described below. Several major city banks, by contrast, will not open any account until you have lived in Japan for six months, or will ask for proof of employment and Japanese-language ability at the counter.
  • Post offices are everywhere. Japan Post Bank works through the post office network, including small towns where no major bank has a branch, and its ATM network is one of the largest in the country.
  • Employers accept it. A Yucho account is a normal Japanese bank account for salary payment purposes. Getting paid is the main job of a first account; everything else can come later.

Once you pass the six-month mark (below), you can open an additional account at a major bank if you want one. Nothing forces you to switch — many long-term residents keep Yucho as their main account for years.

The six-month rule: resident vs. non-resident accounts

Under Japan's foreign exchange law, a person who has been in Japan for less than six months — and some visa categories regardless of duration — is treated as a non-resident (非居住者) for banking purposes. This is the single fact that explains most refusals and restrictions newcomers run into:

Who gets itMost people in their first 6 months in JapanPeople resident in Japan for 6+ months

International transfersRestricted or not availableAvailable (My Number required)
Other servicesSome products and online services unavailableFull range

The practical takeaways: do not be surprised or offended if your first account is a non-resident one — it is a legal classification, not a judgment about you. And after six months, go back and ask to have the account converted to a resident account, especially if you need to send money abroad. If a major bank refuses you entirely in month one, that is also this rule at work; try Japan Post Bank instead of arguing.

Documents checklist

DocumentWhy / notes
Residence card (在留カード)Essential. Your current address must be printed on the back, which means you must complete residence registration first.
Residence registration (住民登録)Register your address at your city or ward office within 14 days of moving in. Without this, almost nothing else in this article is possible.
My Number (マイナンバー)The notification letter or card that arrives after registration. Required for salary-paid accounts in many cases and for international transfers. Bring it if you have it.
PassportSome banks ask for it in addition to the residence card. Bring it — it costs nothing to carry.
Japanese phone numberFrequently required on the application form. This is the classic chicken-and-egg problem; see the SIM guide linked below for how residents solve it.
Personal seal (hanko / inkan) or signatureSome banks register a seal instead of a signature. Japan Post Bank can generally open an account with a signature, but policies vary — if you own a hanko, bring it.
A small cash depositAccounts are opened with an initial deposit; a small amount is fine.

The order that works

  1. City/ward office: register your address and get it printed on your residence card. Your My Number notification follows by post.
  2. Phone number: get a Japanese number — see Long-Term SIM Options for Residents in Japan. Some carriers accept a foreign credit card, which breaks the deadlock before you have a bank account.
  3. Bank: open the Japan Post Bank account with the checklist above.
  4. Employer / landlord / utilities: hand over the account details for salary and direct debits.

Two honest warnings. First, English support at counters is limited outside central Tokyo; bring a translation app and your documents, and expect the process to take a while. Second, if you plan to receive money from abroad in your first months, ask specifically about the non-resident restrictions before relying on the account for that — a separate transfer service may be simpler until your account converts.
