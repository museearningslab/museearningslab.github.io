How to Choose a Japanese Tutor on italki — Advice From a Native Speaker

November 24, 2026

I am a native Japanese speaker (and a language learner myself). Here is how I would pick a Japanese tutor if I were learning Japanese from zero.

I am Japanese, and I am also a student on the other side of the language equation: I am learning English myself, so I know how much the right teacher matters — and how easy it is to pick the wrong one.

Being a native speaker of Japanese gives me one advantage when it comes to italki: I know what good Japanese teaching looks like from the inside. I know which explanations Japanese learners actually need, because I have watched learners struggle with the same three or four walls for years. If you are choosing a Japanese tutor, here is what I would look for.

1. Decide: professional teacher or community tutor?

italki has two kinds of teachers. Professional teachers are licensed or formally trained. Community tutors are native speakers who teach conversation and everyday Japanese.

Neither is automatically better. It depends on your goal:

  • You want structure — grammar in order, JLPT preparation, corrections in writing: choose a professional teacher.
  • You want speaking time — you already study grammar from a textbook and need someone patient to talk with: a community tutor is usually cheaper and perfectly fine.
  • You are a total beginner: start with a professional for the first months. Bad habits in pronunciation and basic grammar are expensive to fix later.

2. Use the trial lesson properly

Most tutors offer a short trial lesson at a low price. Do not waste it on small talk. In the trial, check three things:

  1. Do they correct you? A tutor who only smiles and says "good!" is pleasant and useless. You want gentle, constant correction.
  2. Do they write things down? Good tutors type new words and corrected sentences in the chat or a shared document, so you can review after the lesson.
  3. Do they ask about your goal? "Why are you learning Japanese?" is the first question a serious tutor asks. If they never ask, they are selling time, not teaching.

Take two or three trials with different tutors before committing. It feels slow. It saves months.

3. Read the reviews for the right things

Star ratings are almost all five stars; they tell you little. Read the text of the reviews and look for specifics: "she prepares materials," "he explains grammar in English when I get stuck," "homework is checked every time." Specific praise means real teaching. Vague praise ("nice teacher!") means a nice conversation partner.

Also check how many lessons the tutor has taught. A tutor with 500+ lessons has a system. A new tutor may be excellent and cheaper — but you are the experiment.

4. Match the tutor to your wall

Japanese learners hit predictable walls. Pick a tutor whose profile names yours:

  • Keigo (polite language) for work: look for business Japanese experience.
  • Particles and sentence order: look for grammar-focused professionals.
  • Sounding natural, not textbook-like: look for conversation tutors who promise correction of unnatural phrasing.
  • Kanji: look for tutors who assign reading and writing practice between lessons.

A tutor who claims to teach everything to everyone usually teaches nothing in particular.

5. Price is information, not just cost

Very cheap lessons are not a bargain if you spend six months not improving. As a rough guide, community tutors cost less, professionals cost more, and one focused hour a week with a good tutor beats three unfocused hours with a bad one. Spend on the trial lessons, decide once, then be consistent — switching tutors every week is the most expensive strategy of all, because every first lesson starts from zero.

My honest summary

If I were learning Japanese, I would: take trials with one professional and one community tutor, judge them on correction, notes, and whether they asked about my goal, then book the same tutor weekly for three months before judging the result.
