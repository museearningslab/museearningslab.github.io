Japan IC Card Guide: Suica, PASMO, ICOCA — Which One to Get in 2026

November 25, 2026

Written from Japan. An IC card is the single most useful thing a visitor can buy in Japan: one tap pays for trains, subways, buses, convenience stores, and vending machines. This guide explains the options as they actually stand in late 2026 — including the fact that regular Suica and PASMO cards are back on sale after a two-year shortage.

What an IC card is, in one paragraph

An IC card is a rechargeable smart card. You load it with yen, tap it on the ticket gate when you enter a station, and tap again when you exit — the fare is deducted automatically. The same card also pays at convenience stores, many restaurants and shops, buses, and some taxis. Japan has ten major interoperable IC cards (Suica, PASMO, Kitaca, TOICA, manaca, ICOCA, PiTaPa, Hayakaken, nimoca, and SUGOCA). In practice this means a Suica bought in Tokyo works on trains in Osaka, and an ICOCA bought in Osaka works in Tokyo. You do not need a different card per city. One card per person, though — you cannot pass a single card back through the gate for a second person.

1. Welcome Suica: the default for a short Tokyo trip

Welcome Suica is JR East's card for short-term visitors. There is no deposit: you choose a starting amount of ¥1,000, ¥2,000, ¥3,000, ¥4,000, ¥5,000, or ¥10,000, and the whole amount becomes spendable balance. Two rules matter:

  • It lasts 28 days, counting the day you buy it. The card stops working on day 29 — the "GOOD THRU" date printed on the back is not the expiry date.
  • Leftover balance is never refunded. Load small amounts and top up as you go, rather than loading ¥10,000 on day one.

Since March 27, 2025, it has been sold at dedicated machines at Narita Airport Terminal 1 and Terminal 2·3 stations and Haneda Airport Terminal 3 (Tokyo Monorail) station, and at JR East Travel Service Centers in Tokyo. In principle, one card per person. Child versions exist with half-price fares.

2. Regular Suica and PASMO: back on sale since March 2025

Many older guides still say regular Suica cards are impossible to buy. That was true for a while: a chip shortage stopped sales of unregistered Suica and PASMO cards in June 2023. Sales resumed on March 1, 2025, and as of late 2026 they are sold normally at JR East stations (Suica) and at member railway and bus ticket machines (PASMO).

A regular card costs a ¥500 deposit on top of whatever you load — for example, ¥2,000 buys a card with ¥1,500 of usable balance and a ¥500 deposit. When you leave Japan, return the card at a station of the issuing company and you get the deposit back plus your remaining balance, minus a ¥220 handling fee. If you spend the balance down to zero first, you get the full ¥500 back and lose nothing. A regular card stays valid for 10 years from its last use, so it is the right choice if you plan to come back to Japan, or if your trip is longer than 28 days.

3. The phone options: Apple Wallet and Welcome Suica Mobile

iPhone users have the easiest setup of all. An iPhone 8 or later can add a Suica or PASMO directly in Apple Wallet, with no deposit, no expiry, and top-ups by Apple Pay — you can even set it up before you land. Welcome Suica Mobile, a free iPhone app launched by JR East on March 6, 2025, is the tourist version: no deposit, valid for 180 days, topped up through Apple Pay, and it can also handle Shinkansen and Green Car tickets in the same app. Its balance is not refundable.

Most Android phones sold outside Japan cannot add a Suica, because the system needs Japan's FeliCa NFC standard, which most overseas Android models lack. If you have an Android phone, get a physical card (Welcome Suica or a regular Suica) at the airport instead of fighting with apps.

4. TOURIST PASMO and ICOCA

TOURIST PASMO, launched in May 2026 and sold at Haneda and Narita stations, is PASMO's answer to the Welcome Suica: no deposit, 28 days of validity from purchase, no refunds. If you land in Tokyo and the Welcome Suica machine has a queue, this is an equivalent alternative. ICOCA is JR West's card and the natural choice if your trip is based in Osaka or Kyoto — it works exactly like a regular Suica (¥500 deposit, refundable at JR West stations) and, like Suica, works nationwide on the interoperable network.

5. Top-ups, limits, and where the card does not work

  • Maximum balance: ¥20,000. Do not load more than you can spend.
  • Physical cards top up with cash only at station ticket machines and convenience store registers. Machines accept top-ups in ¥500, ¥1,000, ¥2,000, ¥3,000, ¥5,000, and ¥10,000 increments. Mobile cards top up by card through Apple Pay.
  • Shinkansen (bullet trains) generally need a separate ticket. An IC card covers local trains, subways, and buses, not reserved long-distance trains. If your trip includes the Shinkansen, read our guide on whether the Japan Rail Pass is worth it first — the pass and the IC card solve different problems and most travelers end up using both.
  • Some rural lines do not accept IC cards at all. On small private railways and rural buses, buy a paper ticket. This is rare on the standard tourist routes.

Which one should you get? The decision rule

  • iPhone, any trip length: add Suica in Apple Wallet before you land. Nothing to buy, nothing to return.
  • Android, trip under 28 days: buy a Welcome Suica (or TOURIST PASMO) at the airport. Load ¥2,000–¥3,000 to start and top up as needed.
  • Android, trip over 28 days or repeat visitor: buy a regular Suica or PASMO (¥500 refundable deposit) and return it at the end, or keep it for your next trip.
  • Based in Osaka/Kansai: ICOCA works the same way and is sold at Kansai Airport and JR West stations.

Quick checklist

  1. One card per person — children need their own (child cards charge half fare).
  2. On a tourist card (Welcome Suica / TOURIST PASMO), load small and top up often — leftover money is lost.
  3. On a regular card, spend the balance to zero before returning it to get the full ¥500 deposit back.
  4. Top up physical cards with cash; keep some yen notes for the machines.
  5. Remember the card does not cover the Shinkansen — plan long-distance trains separately.

An IC card will not make your trip cheap on its own — it just removes the friction of buying a ticket for every ride. Pair it with a rough transport budget (see how much a trip to Japan costs) and a data plan for maps (see how to choose an eSIM for Japan), and the logistics of getting around mostly take care of themselves.
