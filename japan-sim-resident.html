Living in Japan

Long-Term SIM Options for Residents in Japan

November 24, 2026

A travel eSIM gets a tourist through two weeks. Living in Japan is a different problem: you need a real Japanese phone number that keeps working — for your bank, your landlord, your employer, and every SMS verification code in between. Here is how resident contracts differ, what you need to sign one, and how to think about budget plans without getting trapped.

This is general guidance, not a plan ranking. Carrier line-ups, campaigns, and prices change constantly, so treat every price question as "check current prices on the carrier's official site" — the thinking below is what stays valid when the numbers move.

Resident SIM vs. tourist eSIM: the differences that matter

Tourist eSIMResident contract (SIM / eSIM)
Japanese phone numberUsually none (data-only)Yes — a real 070 / 080 / 090 number
SMS and verification codesCannot receive regular SMSWorks — banks and services can text you
DurationFixed days, then it expiresMonthly contract, continues until cancelled
Identity checkNone or minimalLegally required identity verification (residence card etc.)
PaymentOne-off card paymentMonthly, from a Japanese bank account or credit card

The phone number is the whole point. In Japan, a mobile number is infrastructure: bank accounts ask for one, rental contracts ask for one, and two-factor authentication assumes one. A data-only tourist eSIM cannot do any of that, which is why "just keep renewing the travel eSIM" stops working the moment you actually live here. For the tourist side of this comparison, see eSIM vs Pocket WiFi vs Roaming in Japan.

What you need to sign a contract

  • Residence card with your registered address — carriers verify identity against it, and the address must match your residence registration.
  • A payment method in your name: a Japanese bank account or a credit card. Some carriers and budget brands accept foreign-issued credit cards, which is often the way to get a number before your bank account exists.
  • My Number — asked for by some carriers and some plan types; bring the notification or card if you have it.
  • An unlocked phone if you are bringing your own device, and eSIM support if you want an eSIM plan rather than a physical SIM.

The chicken-and-egg problem is real: banks want a phone number, carriers want a bank account. The usual escape is to sign the SIM contract first with a credit card, then open the bank account — the full sequence is in How to Open a Bank Account in Japan as a Foreigner.

How to think about budget (MVNO) plans

Japan's budget carriers (格安SIM / MVNOs) rent network space from the major carriers and sell it cheaper, usually with thinner support. Choosing well is mostly about asking the right questions in the right order:

  1. Network first. Budget plans run on one of the major networks (docomo, au, SoftBank, or Rakuten). Coverage where you live and work matters more than headline price — check the coverage map for your actual address.
  2. Voice or data-only? If this is your main number, you need a voice plan. Data-only plans are cheaper but cannot receive bank SMS codes — the exact trap described above.
  3. Honest data amount. Most residents use far less mobile data than they fear, because home and campus Wi-Fi carry the load. Price the plan for your real usage, not your worst month.
  4. Call charges. If you will actually phone people — landlords, offices, delivery drivers — check the per-call rate and whether a flat-rate calling option exists.
  5. Terms and exit. Minimum terms and cancellation fees have largely disappeared from the market, but not universally. Check the contract period, MNP (number transfer) process, and any administration fees before signing.
  6. eSIM and support. If your phone supports eSIM, a plan that offers it can be active the same day. Also check what language support exists — for many budget brands, support is Japanese-only and mostly online.

Well-known options span the major carriers' own budget brands and independent MVNOs; the right one depends on your network, data use, and whether you need same-day eSIM activation. Compare the current line-ups and campaigns on the carriers' official sites — prices and offers change monthly, so any fixed ranking (including on this site) goes stale fast.

Common traps

  • Buying a data-only plan as your main line and discovering at the bank counter that SMS codes cannot reach you.
  • Address mismatch: if the address on your residence card is not your registered current address, identity verification fails. Register the move first.
  • Assuming your home-country card will work: some carriers reject foreign-issued cards. Have a second payment option ready.
  • Letting the travel eSIM lapse before the contract starts: order the resident SIM while the tourist one still works, so you are never unreachable mid-move.
