How to Start Learning Japanese as a Complete Beginner

November 24, 2026

Written by a native Japanese speaker. This is the order I would learn in if I had to start Japanese from zero — what to do first, what to postpone, and where most beginners waste their time.

Japanese looks intimidating from the outside: three writing systems, a completely different grammar order, levels of politeness. But the beginning is more mechanical than people think. The problem is rarely difficulty — it is order. Beginners often start with whatever looks interesting (usually kanji or anime phrases) instead of whatever unlocks everything else. Here is the order that works.

Step 1: Learn kana before anything else

Hiragana and katakana — the two syllabaries, 46 basic characters each — are the foundation. With kana you can read any Japanese word's pronunciation, use a dictionary, and type. Without them, you are stuck with romaji, and romaji quietly teaches you English-shaped pronunciation that you will have to unlearn later.

Most people can learn to recognize both sets in one to two weeks of short daily practice. Write them by hand a few times; your hand remembers what your eyes forget. Do not move on to kanji lists or grammar marathons until kana feels automatic.

Step 2: Build a small, useful core

Next, learn the words and grammar you will actually meet every day:

  • Core vocabulary: a few hundred words — greetings, numbers, time, family, food, common verbs and adjectives. Frequency lists beat random word lists.
  • Basic sentence patterns: Japanese is subject–object–verb, and particles (は, が, を, に, へ, で) mark each word's job. A handful of patterns covers a surprising amount of daily conversation.
  • Polite form first: learn the -ます / です style before casual forms. It is safe in every situation, and casual forms are easier to derive from it than the other way around.

Use spaced repetition (any flashcard app with an SRS algorithm) for vocabulary, in short daily sessions. Twenty steady minutes a day beats a three-hour Sunday session, because memory consolidation happens between sessions, not during them.

Step 3: Add input early — but keep it comprehensible

Once you have kana and a basic core, start feeding your brain real Japanese: graded readers, beginner podcasts, children's shows, or videos with Japanese subtitles. The rule is "slightly above your level." Content you understand 70–80% of teaches you; content you understand 20% of is just noise with good intentions.

Do not wait until you "know enough" to start listening. Your ear needs months of exposure to separate sounds that do not exist in your first language, and that clock only runs while you listen.

Step 4: Start speaking sooner than feels comfortable

This is where most self-taught beginners stall. Apps and textbooks cannot correct your pronunciation, tell you your sentence sounds unnatural, or force you to produce Japanese under time pressure. Speaking is a separate skill from knowledge, and it only grows by speaking.

The practical way to get regular speaking practice is a conversation tutor. On a platform like italki, you can book short lessons with native speakers and practice from your first month — you do not need to be "ready." One or two short sessions a week is enough at the start; the point is feedback and the habit, not volume. If you go this route, I wrote a separate guide on how to choose a Japanese tutor on italki — what to check in trial lessons, reviews, and profiles.

Step 5: Introduce kanji gradually, inside words

Kanji scares beginners because there are thousands of them. In practice, a few hundred cover most everyday text, and you do not need them all at once. Learn kanji inside vocabulary you already know — when you learn the word for "water," learn 水 with it — rather than memorizing isolated characters with long lists of readings. Context gives each character a job; lists just give you anxiety.

A realistic first three months

  • Weeks 1–2: hiragana and katakana until automatic.
  • Month 1: core vocabulary and polite sentence patterns, daily flashcards, first graded listening.
  • Months 2–3: keep the daily core going, add regular input, and start weekly speaking practice — for example with a tutor on italki. Begin kanji inside words you already know.

None of this is glamorous, and that is the point. Japanese rewards consistency far more than intensity. Learn kana, build a small core, listen a lot, speak early, and let kanji accumulate. Do that for three months and you will not be fluent — but you will be a beginner who is actually going somewhere, which is rarer than it should be.
