Japan Tourist Fees 2026–2027: Every New Charge in One Table

November 24, 2026

Japan is raising several travel-related charges in 2026. None of them will ruin a trip on its own — but together they can quietly add up, and most English-language guides have not caught up yet. Here is every confirmed change in one table, with dates, amounts, and what to do about each.

Between March 2026 and November 2026, five separate rule changes affect what visitors pay in Japan: a higher departure tax, a much higher accommodation tax cap in Kyoto, two-tier pricing on the Japan Rail Pass, a complete redesign of tax-free shopping, and — importantly — an entry authorization system (JESTA) that does not exist yet despite what scam sites claim. This article sticks to confirmed facts only. Where a number is not confirmed, it says so.

1. Departure tax: ¥3,000 from July 1, 2026

Japan's departure tax — formally the International Tourist Tax — rises to ¥3,000 per person from July 1, 2026. You never pay it at a counter: it is added to your outbound flight or ferry ticket automatically. The only practical impact is on price comparisons — a "cheap" flight departing Japan after that date already includes the higher tax, so compare total prices, not base fares.

What to do: Nothing. Just know it is inside your ticket price. For a family of four, the increase is already baked into the total you see at checkout.

2. Kyoto accommodation tax: cap rises to ¥10,000 per night

From March 1, 2026, Kyoto City's accommodation tax has a maximum of ¥10,000 per person per night. The tax is tiered by the nightly room charge, so the cap only bites at the luxury end: budget and mid-range stays pay far less. The trap is that many booking sites show the tax separately at checkout or collect it at the property, so a Kyoto stay can look cheaper than it is until the final step.

What to do: When comparing a Kyoto hotel against one in Osaka or Uji, compare the total including accommodation tax. Staying outside Kyoto City and day-tripping in is a legitimate way to avoid the tax entirely — Kyoto is roughly 15–30 minutes by train from Osaka or Otsu (estimates; check your specific route).

3. Japan Rail Pass: two prices from October 1, 2026

From October 1, 2026, the nationwide Japan Rail Pass is sold at two prices: the 7-day pass costs ¥50,000 bought online and ¥53,000 through travel agents. That ¥3,000 gap is real money for zero difference in the product — the pass itself is identical. Whether the pass is worth buying at all is a separate question, and it now fails the math on more itineraries than it used to. I priced three model itineraries in a dedicated article: Is the Japan Rail Pass Worth It in 2027?

What to do: If you buy a pass, buy it online at the official price. Never pay the agent price unless an agent is giving you something else of value in return.

4. Tax-free shopping becomes refund-based on November 1, 2026

This is the change most likely to catch returning visitors off guard. Under the old system, the consumption tax was simply not charged at the register. From November 1, 2026, Japan switches to a refund (pay-first) model: you pay the full price including tax at the store, then claim the tax back through the refund process when you leave Japan, with your purchased goods subject to confirmation at departure.

What to do: Budget for the full shelf price, keep your receipts and purchased items accessible for departure checks, and arrive at the airport with extra time if you plan to claim refunds. Treat the refund as a bonus, not as money you have already saved.

5. JESTA: you do NOT need it in 2027

Japan's electronic travel authorization, JESTA, is planned for introduction in fiscal 2028. For any trip in 2026 or 2027, there is nothing to apply for. Websites that ask you to "apply for JESTA now" and charge a fee are, at best, useless and, at worst, scams harvesting passport details and card numbers.
