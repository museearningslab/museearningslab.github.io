JLPT Levels Explained: Which Level Should You Aim For?

November 24, 2026

The JLPT has five levels and no speaking section. Here is what each level actually means, who needs which one, and how to pick a target that matches your real goal.

The Japanese Language Proficiency Test (JLPT) is the most widely recognized Japanese exam in the world. It has five levels, from N5 (easiest) to N1 (hardest), and it tests vocabulary, grammar, reading, and listening. It does not test speaking or writing — a fact that shapes how you should study for it, and how much you should trust it as a measure of "real" Japanese.

The five levels at a glance

LevelRoughly what it meansTypical use
N5Basic Japanese: hiragana, katakana, around 100 kanji, simple everyday phrases and slow, clear conversation on familiar topics.A first milestone for beginners; proof of serious intent.
N4Basic Japanese, more solid: around 300 kanji, everyday conversation at a slow pace, simple texts on daily topics.Entry point sometimes asked for in language-school and some working-holiday contexts.
N3The bridge level: everyday Japanese in a wider range of situations, natural-ish conversation speed, newspaper headlines and notices.Shows you can function day-to-day; a common target for people living in Japan.
N2Business-usable Japanese: newspapers and magazines on general topics, conversation in most situations, around 1,000 kanji.The level most Japanese employers ask for when they require JLPT at all.
N1Advanced: complex and abstract texts, lectures and news at natural speed, nuance and implication. Around 2,000 kanji.Demanding jobs, translation-adjacent work, and maximum points in some visa point systems.

The kanji and vocabulary counts are approximations commonly used by textbooks, not official quotas — the test does not publish an official word list.

Which level should you aim for? Start from your goal

  • "I just want to enjoy travel and media." Aim for N4–N3. You may never need a certificate at all — but a target level gives your study structure, and N3-level reading opens up everyday Japan in a way phrasebook Japanese cannot.
  • "I want to study in Japan." Requirements vary by institution; many programs look for around N2 for Japanese-taught courses, while language schools take beginners and build you up. Check the specific school's requirements.
  • "I want to work in Japan." N2 is the common benchmark in job postings; N1 helps for roles that are Japanese-first. But remember: the JLPT has no speaking test, and interviews are speaking tests. Certificate plus conversation ability is the real requirement.
  • "I want the strongest possible credential." N1 — with the warning that the jump from N2 to N1 is widely considered the biggest in the whole test. Plan accordingly.

How the test shapes your study (and its blind spot)

Because the JLPT is multiple-choice and receptive, efficient JLPT study is heavy on vocabulary lists, grammar patterns, reading drills, and listening practice with past-paper-style materials. That works — for the test. The blind spot is production: many N2 holders freeze in a real conversation, not because their Japanese is bad, but because they have never practiced speaking under pressure.

The fix is to run two tracks in parallel:

  1. Test track: textbook + vocabulary/kanji review system + timed practice questions, level by level.
  2. Speaking track: regular conversation practice with a tutor who corrects you, so the knowledge you drill actually becomes usable. How long does each level take?

Estimates vary enormously with your native language, study hours, and whether you live in Japan, so treat any number — including these — as a rough orientation: learners studying consistently often take several months to reach N5–N4, around a year or more of steady work to approach N3, and multiple years of cumulative study for N2 and N1. Kanji-background learners (for example, Chinese speakers) usually move faster on reading; everyone moves at the speed of their weekly hours, not their motivation speeches.

A saner way to plan: pick the next level up from where you are, set a test date (the JLPT is held once or twice a year depending on the country), and work backwards. Skipping levels is allowed — you can register directly for any level — but most learners underestimate the gaps, especially N3→N2 and N2→N1.
